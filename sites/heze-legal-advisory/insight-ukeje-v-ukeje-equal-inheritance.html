Ukeje v Ukeje: Equal Inheritance Rights and What They Mean for Estate Planning

The Supreme Court’s 2014 decision remains the leading authority against discrimination in succession. Families and administrators should plan with it in mind.

October 20264 min readHeze Legal Advisory

In Ukeje v Ukeje, decided in 2014, the Supreme Court considered an Igbo customary law rule under which a female child could not share in her deceased father’s estate. The Court held that the rule was discriminatory and conflicted with section 42 of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999, which protects citizens from discrimination on the basis of sex and the circumstances of their birth.

The decision

The Court affirmed that a daughter is entitled to inherit from her father’s estate, and that no customary rule can deprive a child of that right because of gender or the circumstances of birth. A customary rule that does so is void to the extent of its inconsistency with the Constitution.

Why it still matters

More than a decade later, the decision continues to shape estate disputes across the country. Family members excluded from a distribution on the strength of custom can rely on it, and administrators who ignore it risk litigation that delays the estate and erodes its value.

Planning points

  • A properly drafted will remains the clearest way to decide how property passes, and it narrows the room for disputes over custom.
  • Where a person dies without a will, the applicable rules depend on the circumstances, including the form of marriage and any governing customary or Islamic law. Advice should be taken early.
  • Administrators should take the decision into account before distributing assets.
  • Business owners should plan for the succession of company shares and business interests, not only land and personal property.

Families who want certainty about how their estate will be shared should take advice on a will and, where appropriate, a trust.
